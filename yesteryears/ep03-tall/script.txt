The year is 1712 and you are born in a small village in Saxony, in the middle of what will one day be Germany. Your home is a mill on a slow brown river, and it smells of wet stone, warm flour, and the bread your mother bakes every morning before sunrise. Your father is the miller. He is a short, stocky, practical man who believes in hard work, fair weights, and never trusting anyone from the city. Your mother is even shorter. She bakes, she sews, she keeps the accounts, and she can silence a room with one look.

You are born an ordinary size. Everyone in the village agrees you are a perfectly normal baby.

That doesn't last.

By age 5, you are taller than the other children. By age 10, you are taller than your father. By age 14, you are taller than anyone in the village, including the blacksmith, who used to be famous for it. You bang your head on every doorway in your house so many times that your mother hangs a little bell above the kitchen door to warn you.

Every morning, as you duck under it, she says the same thing. Mind your head, love.

You always do. Mostly.

Being tall is wonderful at first. You can carry two sacks of flour at once. You can reach the apples nobody else can reach. At the harvest festival, you win the log-carrying contest three years in a row, and the smaller children follow you around the square like ducklings. Your father jokes that he'll never need a ladder again. Then he stops buying ladders, just to prove his point. When the roof of the mill needs fixing, he simply points at you.

But your mother doesn't laugh at that joke. She doesn't like it when travellers stop at the mill and stare at you for too long. And she especially doesn't like it when they ask how tall you are.

She tells you to slouch around strangers. You don't understand why.

You find out in 1730.

You're 18 years old, and you've just grown again. You're now well over six feet tall, a true giant in a time when most men barely reach five and a half. One autumn afternoon, your father sends you to the market town two days away to sell a cart of flour. It's your first trip alone.

Your mother packs you bread and sausage and walks you to the road. She grips your arm, looks up at you, and says the strangest thing. If anyone offers you work, a drink, or a ride, you say no.

You laugh. You promise.

You sell the flour by noon. You get a good price. You decide to celebrate with one drink at the inn before heading home. Just one.

Big mistake.

The man who sits down beside you is friendly, well-dressed, and very interested in you. He buys you a beer. Then another. He tells you he's looking for a strong young man to work as a footman for a wealthy nobleman. Fine clothes. Good food. Wages three times what a miller's son could ever dream of.

You remember your mother's warning. You say no, thank you.

He smiles and says that's a shame. Then he buys you one more beer.

You don't remember drinking it.

You wake up in the back of a moving cart with your hands tied, your head pounding, and a sack over your face. You hear wheels on a dirt road. Men laughing. Someone kicks your ribs and tells you to stop struggling, because the King doesn't like damaged goods.

You aren't alone. When the cart stops and someone pulls the sack off your head, you see two other young men tied up beside you. A shepherd from Hanover. A student from Leipzig. Both of them are enormous. Both of them look as terrified as you feel. The student keeps insisting there's been a mistake, that his father is a lawyer. Nobody listens.

You are being smuggled across the border.

You have just been kidnapped by Prussian recruiters.

To understand why, you need to understand the man on the Prussian throne. His name is Frederick William the First, and he is obsessed with exactly one thing. Tall soldiers.

He has created a special regiment called the Potsdam Giants. A personal guard of the tallest men he can find in Europe, and he will do absolutely anything to get them. He pays fortunes. He trades for them. Other kings send him tall men as gifts to win his favour. And when buying doesn't work, his recruiters simply take them. Off the streets. Out of taverns. From farms and churches and even from foreign countries, where kidnapping a man is very much against the law.

The King doesn't care about the law. He cares about height.

And you, unfortunately, have plenty of it.

The cart rolls for days. Then you arrive in Potsdam.

The first week is disorienting in the best possible way. They untie you. They feed you. And the food is good. Meat every day. White bread. Beer. They bathe you, shave you, and measure you with a long wooden rod. A clerk writes down your height in a thick ledger, and an officer in a gold-trimmed coat whistles in admiration.

Then they give you your uniform.

It's the finest thing you've ever worn. A blue coat with red cuffs and brass buttons, white trousers, polished boots. And on your head, a tall pointed grenadier's cap, made of brass and red cloth, that adds almost another foot to your height. When you see yourself in the mirror, you barely recognize the giant looking back.

Your pay is more than your father earns in a good month. Your barracks are clean. The officers call the giants the King's darlings.

On Sundays, the regiment parades through the streets of Potsdam, a town built almost entirely for soldiers. Townspeople line the road to watch. Children run beside you, craning their necks, pointing at your cap. An old woman throws a flower at your boots. For the first time in your life, being tall feels like being famous.

For a few days, you let yourself think, maybe this isn't so terrible.

Then the drilling starts.

Every day, from dawn until your legs shake, you march. You turn. You present arms. You march again. Hundreds of giants moving as one perfect machine, while a short, red-faced sergeant screams at you to stand up straight. Any mistake is punished with a wooden cane across the back. Too slow, the cane. Too fast, the cane. Out of step, the cane.

And you can't leave.

Not on your own. Not to visit home. Not ever. You belong to the King now, as completely as his horses or his furniture.

You write to your mother. You don't know if your letters ever reach her.

The man in the bunk next to yours is called Seamus. He's an Irishman, even taller than you, with flaming red hair, a crooked nose, and a laugh you can hear from across the parade ground. He was tricked onto a ship in Dublin by a man who promised him a job as a servant to an ambassador. He's been in Potsdam for two years, and he still hasn't learned to speak German properly. He doesn't need to. He communicates almost entirely through jokes and rude hand gestures.

He's the first person in Potsdam who makes you laugh.

On your first night, he shows you the wooden beam above the barracks door. It's covered in carved notches, each one marked with a name and a height. Every giant who has ever slept in this room has cut his mark there. Seamus's notch is the highest one on the beam, and he is unbearably proud of it. He hands you his knife. You carve your mark two inches below his. He tells you that's exactly where you belong.

You hadn't realized how long it had been since you laughed.

Seamus teaches you how to survive. How to keep your boots polished so the sergeant can see his own ugly face in them. How to stand perfectly still for an hour by picking a spot on the horizon and pretending it owes you money. How to hide a sausage in your grenadier cap for later. He teaches you Irish drinking songs. You teach him German swear words. Within a month, he's using them on the sergeant, very quietly, while smiling.

At night, when the barracks are dark, he tells you about Ireland. Green hills. Grey sea. A mother who sings while she cooks, just like yours. He says that one day, he's going home. You ask him how. He just taps his nose and grins.

Then you meet the King.

It happens on your third month, on a cold morning parade. A short, heavy man in a plain blue uniform walks down the line, leaning on a cane. His face is puffy and red. His eyes are small and sharp. He looks nothing like a king.

But everyone around you freezes.

He stops in front of you. He looks up, and up, and up. Then he smiles like a child on Christmas morning. He pats your arm. He asks your name, your village, your height. He tells the officer beside him that you're a splendid one. A real beauty.

Then he moves on to the next giant.

Seamus whispers that you've been lucky. The King has a famous temper. He beats people with that cane in the street. Ministers. Merchants. His own children. If he's in a bad mood at parade, somebody always ends up bleeding.

Over the next months, you learn the King's strange habits. He paints portraits of his giants, sitting at his easel for hours, even when he's sick. He orders his tallest men to marry tall women, hoping to breed even taller soldiers. And when he's ill and confined to his bed, he sometimes orders the whole regiment to march through his bedroom, in full uniform, just to cheer himself up.

You do this twice. Two hundred giants, stooping through palace doorways, marching past a sick man in his nightshirt, who claps and cries with joy.

Mind your head, love.

You almost laugh. Almost.

And then you notice what the King never does. He never sends his giants to war.

Not once.

All the drilling. All the canes. All the kidnapping. The Potsdam Giants are too precious to risk in battle. You exist only to be looked at. You're not soldiers. You're a collection.

Like stamps. Like paintings. Like toys.

Seamus figures this out long before you do. And one night in your second winter, he tells you his secret. He's leaving. There's a cart leaving for Hamburg in the morning, hidden under barrels of salted fish, and from Hamburg, ships go to Ireland. He's paid the driver everything he has.

He asks you to come with him.

You think about it. You think about the mill and the river and your mother's bell above the door. You want to say yes more than you've ever wanted anything.

But you've heard what happens to deserters.

You say no.

Seamus nods. He doesn't argue. He just claps you on the shoulder, tells you you're a big soft German potato, and climbs out of the window into the snow.

You lie awake until dawn, listening for shouting. It doesn't come. For three days, there's no news. On the fourth day, you let yourself believe he's made it.

On the fifth day, they bring him back.

The fish cart was searched at a checkpoint. The driver sold him out for a reward. Seamus is dragged back into Potsdam in chains, his red hair matted, his crooked nose broken again.

His punishment is called running the gauntlet.

Two long lines of soldiers face each other across the parade ground. Two hundred men, each holding a thin, flexible rod. The prisoner is stripped to the waist and forced to walk slowly between the lines, while every soldier strikes his back as he passes. Once. Then back again. Then again.

You are ordered to stand in the line.

You are handed a rod.

When Seamus reaches you, he looks up at you. He doesn't say anything. He doesn't need to. You raise the rod and bring it down as softly as you dare, and the sergeant sees, and you know you'll pay for that later. You don't care.

You pay for it the next morning. The sergeant canes you in front of the whole regiment, twenty strokes, slow and careful, while the King's officers watch. You stand up straight the entire time. You don't make a sound. Seamus would have been proud.

Seamus walks the line eight times. On the eighth, he falls and doesn't get up.

They carry him to the infirmary. He dies there three days later. He's 26 years old.

They bury him outside the city, in an unmarked grave, without a single song.

You don't cry. You've stopped crying. You go back to the barracks and stand under the beam above the door. His notch is still there, the highest one, with his name carved crookedly beside it. Nobody else ever looks at it.

You go back to your bunk and polish your boots until you can see your own face in them. It's not a face you recognize anymore.

The years pass the way they do in Potsdam. Drill. Parade. Cane. Drill. You don't try to escape. You don't make new friends. You stand perfectly still for hours, staring at a spot on the horizon, pretending it owes you money.

Your joints start to ache. Many of the giants are sick, you realize. Some of them have grown so tall because something is wrong inside them. Their bones hurt. Their hearts struggle. They die young, one by one, and new giants arrive to fill their places, kidnapped from farms and inns and ships, just like you.

By 1740, you are 28. You've been a giant in a cage for ten years.

Then, in the spring, the King gets sick. Truly sick this time. His body swells. He can't walk. He can barely breathe. And one last time, he orders his giants to march past his bed. You stoop through the doorway. You look at the bloated old man propped up on his pillows, and he looks up at you with wet, adoring eyes.

He doesn't know your name. He never did.

On May 31st, 1740, Frederick William the First dies.

The new king is his son, the future Frederick the Great, a man who loves flutes, poetry, and real armies. He looks at the Potsdam Giants and sees only an enormous waste of money. Within weeks, the regiment is broken up. Most of the giants are reassigned to ordinary units. Some are simply sent away.

You are one of them.

One morning, an officer hands you your discharge papers and a few coins and tells you that you're free to go.

Free.

You stand in the street outside the barracks in your old civilian clothes, which are now far too small. You don't know what to do with your hands. Nobody is screaming at you. Nobody is holding a cane. For ten years, every minute of your life belonged to someone else.

So you do the only thing you can think of. You walk home.

It takes you three weeks. You sleep in barns and under hedges. You trade your last coins for bread. Your knees ache. Your back aches. Children stare at you in every village, and you instinctively stand up straight before you remember that you don't have to anymore.

When you finally reach the mill, it looks smaller than you remember. The wheel still turns. The river is still brown. Your father is standing in the yard, older now, greyer, a little bent. He looks up at you for a long time without saying anything.

Then he tells you your mother died two winters ago. She wrote to you every month for ten years. Not one of her letters ever arrived.

He shows you the kitchen. Above the door, the little bell is still hanging there. Nobody ever took it down.

You duck under it.

And for the first time in ten years, you cry.

The next morning, your father takes out his old knife. He stands you against the kitchen doorframe, right beside the little bell, the way he used to when you were a boy. He reaches up as high as he can, stands on a stool, and carves a notch at the top of your head.

Then, without saying a word, he carves a second notch, two inches higher. You ask him who that one's for. He says, for your friend. You never told him about Seamus. But last night, through the thin wall, he heard you say the name in your sleep.

And here's the part nobody tells you.

At its peak, the King's giant regiment had around three thousand men. Recruiting them cost Prussia a fortune, and a single exceptional giant could cost the King more than a small estate. Russia's Peter the Great once sent him dozens of tall men as a gift. Other rulers did the same, hoping to stay on the King's good side.

And the Potsdam Giants never fought a single battle. Not one. Across nearly three decades, thousands of men were bought, traded, tricked, and kidnapped from all over Europe, drilled with canes, punished to death for trying to leave, all for a regiment that existed only so one man could look at it.

Today, the king's own paintings of his giants still hang in palace collections. Visitors stand in front of them and laugh at how strange it all was. Some of them stand next to the portraits and measure themselves against the painted giants, just for fun.

And in your world, being tall is a gift. Tall people play professional basketball. They walk runways. They get asked to reach the top shelf at the supermarket, and the worst thing that happens is a bumped head on a low doorway.

Nobody comes for you in the night. Nobody measures you with a wooden rod and writes your height in a ledger.

Mind your head, love.

That's the only warning you'll ever need.
