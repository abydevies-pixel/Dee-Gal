The year is 505, and you are born in Constantinople, the greatest city in the world. Capital of the Roman Empire in the east. Half a million people, packed between the sea and walls so thick no army has ever broken through them. Your home is a small room above a fish stall near the harbour on the Golden Horn, and it smells of salt, seaweed, and yesterday’s mackerel. Your father sells fish. He is a loud, cheerful, broad-shouldered man who believes in three things. God, the Emperor, and the Greens.

Mostly the Greens.

Because in Constantinople, the most important question about any person isn’t their job, or their family, or even their religion. It’s this. Blue or Green?

The Blues and the Greens are the city’s two great chariot-racing teams. But calling them teams doesn’t come close. They are tribes. Clubs. Political parties. Street gangs. Every neighbourhood, every guild, every family picks a side, and they stay on it for generations. Your grandfather was Green. Your father is Green. The day you’re born, your father wraps you in a green cloth and holds you up to the window so the whole street can see.

Then he says the words you’ll hear every day of your life. “Green till we die”.

Your mother rolls her eyes. She’s heard it a thousand times. She says it anyway.

“Green till we die”.

By age 4, you can chant every Green song by heart. By age 6, your father takes you to your first race.

You’ll never forget it.

The Hippodrome is the biggest building you’ve ever seen. A giant stone racetrack, longer than four fields, surrounded by tiers of seats so high they seem to touch the sky. It holds a hundred thousand people. Down the middle of the track runs a long stone barrier covered with ancient treasures. An Egyptian obelisk. A bronze column of twisted snakes. Statues of famous charioteers. At one end, connected directly to the Great Palace, is the royal box, where the Emperor himself sits to watch.

And the noise. You’ve never heard anything like it. A hundred thousand voices, half of them singing Blue songs, half of them singing Green, each side trying to drown out the other.

Your father lifts you onto his shoulders. Four chariots burst from the starting gates, four horses each, wheels screaming on the sand. Blue. Green. Red. White. Seven laps around the barrier, at speeds that make the crowd scream. On the third lap, two chariots collide at the turn and explode into splinters. The crowd roars like the sea.

The Green chariot wins.

Your father throws you into the air. Strangers hug you. A toothless old man kisses your forehead. You’ve never felt anything like it in your life. You belong to something enormous.

You’re Green. Till you die.

That night, your father carves you a little wooden chariot, painted green, with four wobbly horses. You race it along the floor until your mother hides it under the bed so she can sleep. You find it in the morning and race it again.

Your hero is a charioteer called Porphyrius, the greatest racer of the age. The city has built statues of him on the barrier, in solid bronze, while he’s still alive. You decide you’ll be just like him one day. Your father laughs and says you smell too much like fish to drive a chariot.

Years pass, and the races become your whole life. You learn the names of every horse. You argue about charioteers with the other boys on the docks. You save every coin you earn gutting fish for your father, and spend it all on race days.

And across the alley, in the Blue bakery, there’s Leon.

Leon is the baker’s son. He’s your age, skinny, quick-witted, with flour permanently stuck in his eyebrows. And he is, unfortunately, a Blue. A fanatical one. The first time you meet, you’re both seven years old, and you get into a fistfight over whose charioteer has better hair.

You lose. Leon gives you a bread roll afterwards to make up for it.

He becomes your best friend.

It makes no sense, and everyone tells you so. Your father calls him that flour-faced Blue. Leon’s father calls you the fish boy. But every race day for ten years, you and Leon walk to the Hippodrome together, arguing the whole way, split up at the entrance to sit with your own side, and meet again afterwards to argue all the way home. The loser pays for dinner.

You lose more often than you’d like to admit.

And when you’re sixteen, Leon proves what kind of friend he is. One night after a race, three Blue thugs corner you in an alley near the harbour. They’ve seen your green scarf. They’re bigger than you. One of them is holding a club.

Then Leon steps out of the shadows behind them. He tells them you’re his cousin. He tells them you’re colour blind, and that your mother dresses you. They look at each other. They look at him. Then they shrug and walk away.

You don’t speak to him for a week out of pure embarrassment. Then you buy him dinner.

By the time you’re 18, being a fan isn’t just about racing anymore.

The factions run the city now. The hardcore young members, the partisans, have their own look. They shave the front of their heads and grow the back long, the way the Huns do. They grow wild beards. They wear tunics with enormous puffed sleeves, tight at the wrist, and they swagger through the streets in gangs, singing.

You join the Green partisans the day after your eighteenth birthday. You shave your hair. You get your puffed sleeves. When you walk down the street with your brothers, people step aside. Shopkeepers give you free bread. Nobody dares overcharge a Green.

Leon on the other hand joins the Blues on the same day. He shows up at your fish stall with half a shaved head and sleeves so big he can barely fit through the door. You both laugh until you cry.

Then the nights start.

At first it’s just shouting matches. Blue gangs and Green gangs, meeting on street corners after the races, trading insults. Then rocks. Then sticks. Then one night, someone in your gang pulls out a knife.

You learn quickly that the factions don’t just fight each other. They rob people in the street. They break into shops. Partisans walk around at night with weapons hidden in their giant sleeves, and the city watch is afraid to stop them. The factions are too powerful. Too many friends in high places. And the people with the friends in the highest places are the Blues.

One night, your gang breaks into a spice merchant’s shop. You stand guard at the door. You don’t take anything. You tell yourself that makes it different. But you hear the old merchant pleading inside, and you don’t go in to stop it. You go home and lie awake listening to your father snore, and you don’t tell him.

In 527, a new emperor takes the throne. His name is Justinian. He’s brilliant, ambitious, hardworking, and has supported the Blues for years. His wife, the Empress Theodora, is a former actress who grew up around the Hippodrome. Her family once worked for the Greens, until the Greens refused to help them. She’s never forgiven them.

So when a Blue partisan commits a crime, the judges look the other way. When a Green does the same thing, he hangs.

You don’t say this to Leon. You don’t have to. He knows.

Meanwhile, things are getting harder for everyone. Justinian is fighting wars in Persia and needs money, and his tax minister, a ruthless man called John the Cappadocian, squeezes every coin out of the city. Your father’s fish stall now pays twice the tax it did five years ago. Leon’s father has had to let two bakers go. People are hungry. People are angry.

And for the first time, the Blues and the Greens are angry about the same thing.

Your father is getting old now. His back hurts. His shoulders, the ones you sat on at your first race, are stooped. He still goes to every race. He still sings every song. But one evening, he looks at your shaved head and your puffed sleeves and the bruise on your cheek, and he says quietly that the Greens are supposed to be about the horses. Not this.

You tell him he doesn’t understand. He just nods and goes back to gutting fish.

In January 532, you’re 27 years old, and everything goes wrong at once.

After a riot following the races, the city prefect arrests a group of men from both factions for murder. Seven are condemned to death. On the morning of the execution, a huge crowd gathers to watch.

The first hanging goes as planned. Then the rope breaks on the next two. Not once, but twice. One of the condemned men is a Blue. The other is a Green. Both survive the fall.

The crowd goes wild. It’s a sign from God, they shout. Some monks rush in, grab the two survivors, and smuggle them across the water into the church of Saint Lawrence, where nobody can arrest them. Soldiers surround the church. Nobody moves.

On January 13th, the races go ahead as planned. The Emperor takes his seat in the royal box. And the whole Hippodrome, Blue and Green alike, begins chanting at him.

Mercy. Mercy for the two men.

Justinian doesn’t answer.

Race after race, the chanting gets louder. Justinian ignores them. By the twenty-second race, something happens that has never happened in the history of Constantinople.

The Blues and the Greens start chanting together.

One word. Over and over.

“Nika”

It means win. Conquer. It’s what you shout to cheer on your charioteer. But now, a hundred thousand people are shouting it at the Emperor, in one voice, Blue and Green as one.

You look across the stands and see Leon, standing on his seat, screaming the same word you are. For the first time in your lives, you’re on the same side.

It feels incredible.

The crowd pours out of the Hippodrome and into the streets. They break into the prisons and free the prisoners. They set fire to the city prefect’s headquarters. You’re swept along with them, chanting, laughing, carrying torches. Leon is beside you. You slap each other on the back. You can’t believe how powerful it feels. The Emperor can’t ignore you now.

Then the fires spread.

The wind catches them. By the next day, half the centre of the city is burning. The Senate house. Public baths. Houses. Shops. The great church of Hagia Sophia, the most important church in the Empire, burns to the ground. People who had nothing to do with the riot lose everything.

You walk past a family sitting in the street in front of the smoking ruins of their home. A little girl is crying. Her mother looks at your puffed Green sleeves with pure hatred.

You stop chanting.

Leon notices. He asks if you’re alright. You say yes. You’re not.

That night, the two of you climb onto the roof of your father’s fish stall and watch the city burn. The sky over the palace is orange. You can hear the roar of the crowds and the crash of collapsing roofs. Leon doesn’t make a single joke. For the first time since you were seven years old, neither of you can think of anything to say.

Your father calls up from the street. He tells you to come home. To stay home. He says this isn’t a race anymore.

You tell him you’ll be careful. He looks at you for a long time. Then he goes back inside.

The riot goes on for five days. The Emperor tries to calm the crowd. He fires John the Cappadocian. He fires his other unpopular ministers. It isn’t enough anymore. The crowd doesn’t want reforms. It wants a new emperor.

On the fifth day, they find one. An old aristocrat called Hypatius, a nephew of a former emperor, who has been hiding in his house, hoping nobody would remember him. The crowd drags him out, carries him to the Hippodrome, and crowns him with a golden chain in the royal box, in front of tens of thousands of cheering people.

You’re there. You cheer too. You don’t know what else to do.

Hypatius looks terrified. He looks like a man who already knows how this ends.

Inside the palace, you’ll learn much later, Justinian is getting ready to run. A ship is waiting to carry him away. His advisers tell him it’s over.

But the Empress Theodora stands up and makes a speech that will be repeated for fifteen hundred years. She says she’d rather die an empress than live as a refugee. That royalty makes a fine burial shroud. That she will not run.

Justinian stays.

And he makes a plan.

That afternoon, while you’re in the Hippodrome with the crowd, a court official called Narses quietly walks into the Blue section. He’s carrying bags of gold. He speaks to the Blue leaders. He reminds them that Justinian has always been a Blue. That Hypatius might favour the Greens. That this is not their fight.

You watch the Blues begin to stand up. One by one. Then hundreds of them. Then thousands. They’re leaving.

Leon finds you in the Green stands. He grabs your arm. He looks scared, and Leon is never scared. He says you need to come with him. Right now. He says something bad is going to happen.

You look at him. “Green till we die”, you tell him.

Leon stares at you for a long moment. Then he lets go of your arm. He doesn’t say goodbye. He just disappears into the crowd of departing Blues.

You turn back to the track. A few minutes later, the Hippodrome gates slam shut.

At first, nobody understands. Then you hear it. The sound of metal. Marching feet. And soldiers, real soldiers, veterans of the wars against Persia, pour in through the entrances. At their head is the Emperor’s best general, Belisarius.

There’s nowhere to run. The gates are sealed. The walls are too high. A hundred thousand people were cheering here an hour ago. Now there are only screams.

You don’t remember much of what happens next. Only noise, and pushing, and the sand of the racetrack under your feet. The same sand where you watched the Green chariot win when you were six years old, sitting on your father’s shoulders.

You never leave the Hippodrome.

By evening, everywhere is silent.

The historian Procopius, who lived through those days, wrote that about thirty thousand people died inside the Hippodrome that afternoon. Thirty thousand sports fans. In a few hours.

The next day, they execute Hypatius. The riot is over. Justinian keeps his throne for another thirty-three years, and the races start again within a few years. The Blues and the Greens keep competing for centuries.
