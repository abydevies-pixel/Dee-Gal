The year is 1600 and you are born in Istanbul, capital of the Ottoman Empire, the most powerful empire on earth. Your home is a narrow wooden house in Tahtakale, the old market district near the Golden Horn, and it smells of something most of the world has never smelled before. Roasting coffee.

Your father runs a coffeehouse. A small one, with a blue door, six cushions along the wall, and a copper pot that never stops bubbling. He is a gentle, round, patient man with a burn on every finger and a belief that any problem in the world can be solved with a good cup and a long conversation. Your mother keeps the accounts and complains about the cushions. She says the customers never leave.

She's right. Nobody ever leaves a coffeehouse.

Coffee is still new in Istanbul. Fifty years before you're born, two merchants from Syria opened the first coffeehouse in this very neighbourhood. People thought they were mad. A hot black drink made from roasted beans from Yemen? Bitter as medicine? Who would pay for that?

Everyone, it turns out.

Within a few decades, there are hundreds of coffeehouses across the city. People call them schools of the wise. Poets read their new verses there. Merchants make deals. Old men play backgammon for hours. Puppeteers put on shadow plays. Storytellers perform until midnight. For the first time in history, ordinary people have somewhere to go that isn't the mosque, the market, or home. Somewhere to sit, and talk, and think.

Your father says coffee doesn't just wake up the body. It wakes up the mind.

You take your first sip at age 7. It's the most disgusting thing you've ever tasted. You spit it across the floor, and the whole coffeehouse bursts out laughing. Your father pats your head and tells you to try again tomorrow.

You do. And the day after that.

By age 10, you can't live without it.

You spend your days behind the counter, washing tiny cups and listening. The men on the cushions argue about everything. The price of bread. The war with Persia. Whether a poem written yesterday is genius or garbage. Your father never takes a side. He just refills the cups and winks at you when the arguments get loud. A coffeehouse is a ship, he tells you. The keeper is just the captain. He doesn't steer the conversation. He keeps it afloat.

By age 12, you're roasting the beans yourself. You learn to shake the pan over the coals until the beans turn the colour of chestnuts and start to crackle like rain on a roof. You learn to grind them into powder as fine as flour. You learn to boil the coffee three times in the little copper pot, with a pinch of cardamom, until a thick foam rises on top. Your father says the foam is the soul of the coffee. No foam, no soul.

And you discover you have a gift. Your nose. You can smell a pot from three streets away and tell if it's burnt. You can tell beans from the port of Mocha from beans that have sat too long on a ship. Your father starts calling you the little sultan of the coffee pot.

Your mother tells you not to say the word sultan so loudly.

You don't understand why.

You will.

Every evening, when the lamps are lit, the most important man in your world arrives at the blue door. His name is Mustafa Efendi, and he's a storyteller. A meddah. He's old and thin, with a white beard that reaches his belly, a voice like a whole theatre company, and exactly two props. A handkerchief and a wooden stick.

With those two things, Mustafa can become anyone. A sultan. A thief. A jealous camel. A ghost. He taps the stick on the floor and the room goes silent. He waves the handkerchief and suddenly he's a princess, a pasha, a sea captain in a storm. He can make grown janissaries cry and old merchants laugh so hard they spill their coffee.

His stories go on for weeks, and he always stops at the most exciting moment. Come back tomorrow, he says, and everyone groans, and everyone comes back tomorrow.

You fill his cup for free. Every night. He's the reason half the customers come.

One winter, he tells a story about a fisherman who catches a magic fish that grants three wishes. It goes on for nineteen nights. On the eighteenth night, the fisherman is about to make his final wish, and Mustafa stops, sips his coffee, and says, come back tomorrow. A carpet seller in the corner throws his slipper at him. Mustafa catches it without looking and puts it in his pocket. He refuses to give it back until the carpet seller buys a round for the whole room.

Mustafa likes you. He says you have a storyteller's ears. Sometimes, after closing, he teaches you the voices. The pasha. The camel. The ghost. You practise them in the kitchen until your mother throws a spoon at you.

He becomes your favourite person in the city.

And he tells you something you never forget. A story is like coffee, he says. Too fast, and it burns. Too slow, and it goes cold. The trick is to stop just before they've had enough. That way, they always come back tomorrow.

Years pass, and in 1623, a new sultan takes the throne. His name is Murad the Fourth, and he's eleven years old.

A child sultan. The real power is held by his mother and the generals, and the city is chaos. The janissaries, the Sultan's elite soldiers, riot whenever they're unhappy with their pay. Ministers are murdered in the streets. And a lot of the plotting, the rumours, and the complaints about the government happen in exactly one kind of place.

Coffeehouses.

At the same time, a group of strict preachers are growing more powerful in the mosques. They say coffee is a sinful innovation. That it's like wine. That coffeehouses are nests of gossip, laziness, and rebellion, and that a good Muslim should be at prayer, not sitting on a cushion arguing about politics.

Your father laughs it off. People have been complaining about coffee since the day it arrived, he says. Nothing ever comes of it.

In 1625, your father dies in his sleep, with a cup of coffee cold on the table beside him. You're 25 years old, and the blue door is yours.

The first year is disorienting in the best possible way. Business is booming. You add four more cushions. You buy a second copper pot. You hire a boy to grind the beans. Mustafa's stories pack the room so tight that people stand outside in the street just to listen through the window. Your mother finally stops complaining about the cushions and starts complaining about the crowds instead.

For a few years, you let yourself think that life will always be like this.

Then the boy sultan grows up.

By 1632, Murad is twenty years old, and he's done being a puppet. He's enormously strong. They say he can throw a heavy iron mace further than any soldier in the army, and that he wrestles champions for fun. He takes control of the empire with terrifying speed. He has rebellious janissaries executed. He has corrupt officials executed. He has anyone who looks like a threat executed. The city learns very quickly that this sultan doesn't make speeches. He makes examples.

And he's listening to the strict preachers.

Then, in the summer of 1633, a great fire tears through Istanbul. It burns for days. Thousands of houses are destroyed, whole neighbourhoods turned to ash. People need someone to blame. And somebody whispers to the Sultan that the fire was a sign. A punishment. That the city has become sinful. That people sit around in coffeehouses gossiping against their ruler instead of praying.

A few weeks later, the announcement is read in every square in the city.

Coffee is banned. So is tobacco. So is wine. Every coffeehouse in Istanbul is to be closed and destroyed.

You hear the news in your own coffeehouse. The room goes silent. Mustafa, for once in his life, has nothing to say.

The soldiers arrive the next morning.

They don't knock. They kick the blue door off its hinges. They throw the cushions into the street. They smash your copper pots. They carry out every sack of beans you own and pile them in the middle of the road with the beans from every other coffeehouse in Tahtakale. Then they set the whole pile on fire.

The smell is overwhelming. For a whole afternoon, the entire district smells like the best coffee in the world. People stand in the street with tears in their eyes, breathing it in.

Your mother holds your hand. She doesn't say I told you so. She doesn't have to.

By evening, the blue door is firewood.

The next few weeks are miserable in a way you never expected. Your head pounds from morning to night. You can't think. You can't sleep. You snap at your mother over nothing. You didn't know a drink could leave a hole like that. Half the city is walking around with the same headache, the same short temper, the same look of quiet suffering. Nobody says why. Nobody needs to.

And the punishments are real. The Sultan isn't joking. The rumours spread fast. People caught drinking coffee are beaten in public. People caught a second time, the stories say, are sewn into leather bags and thrown into the Bosphorus.

And the worst rumour of all is true. The Sultan himself walks the streets at night. In disguise. Dressed as an ordinary man, with an executioner following a few steps behind. Anyone he catches smoking or drinking is dealt with on the spot.

Nobody knows what he looks like in plain clothes. That's the point. He could be anyone. The man next to you at the market. The stranger asking for directions. Anyone.

So the city goes quiet. The streets empty at sunset. People stop gathering in groups. People stop talking about the Sultan at all.

And you do something incredibly stupid.

You keep making coffee.

You hide a small copper pot and a sack of beans under the floorboards of your mother's house. Once a week, late at night, you roast a handful of beans with all the windows shut and wet cloths hanging over the cracks so the smell can't escape. Then you pour a few cups for your most trusted friends. Five of them. Sitting on the floor of a back room, in the dark.

And Mustafa tells stories. In a whisper. He does the camel's voice so quietly that you have to lean in until your foreheads almost touch. He does the ghost so softly it gives you chills. And at the end of every night, at the most exciting moment, he stops, finishes his cup, and whispers, come back tomorrow.

You have rules. Nobody arrives together. Nobody leaves together. Nobody laughs out loud. The cups are rinsed and hidden before the last guest goes home. The grounds are buried in the garden. If anyone ever asks, you were praying.

It's the most dangerous thing you've ever done. And it's the happiest you've been in months.

But Mustafa is old. And old storytellers are hard to keep quiet.

One night in the spring of 1634, he gets carried away. He's telling a story about a foolish, cruel king, and he's doing the voice, and the voice gets louder, and louder, and someone outside hears it. And someone outside is listening very carefully.

They come for him the next day. Not at your house. At his. They find a cup with coffee grounds in it on his windowsill. And a few leaves of tobacco in his pocket. And a story about a foolish, cruel king that everyone in the neighbourhood heard him tell.

The last thing he ever said to you, the night before, as he slipped out into the dark, was come back tomorrow.

You never see him again.

They say it was quick. They say he didn't beg. They say that right at the end, he tapped his stick once on the ground, the way he did before every story, and the soldiers went quiet without knowing why.

You don't know if that last part is true. You decide to believe it.

You bury the copper pot in the garden that night. You don't make coffee for a long time after that.

The years go by in silence. No coffeehouses. No stories. No foam. You work as a porter in the market, carrying sacks of other people's spices. Your mother grows old. Sometimes, late at night, you take out Mustafa's handkerchief, which his family gave you after he was gone, and you practise the voices quietly to yourself. The pasha. The camel. The ghost.

Then, one cold night in 1637, there's a knock on your door.

It's very late. Your mother is asleep. You open the door a crack. Standing in the street is a tall young man in plain, dusty robes, like a traveller. He has a neat black beard and calm, dark eyes. Behind him, at the end of the street, another man waits in the shadows.

The stranger smiles. He says he's a merchant from Bursa. That he's lost, and cold, and tired. And then he says it.

He's heard that in this street, a man might still find a good cup of coffee.

Your heart stops.

You think about the copper pot buried in the garden. You think about the smell of roasting beans. You think about how long it's been since you tasted anything that wasn't bread and water. For one second, just one, you want to say yes more than you've ever wanted anything.

Then you think about Mustafa. And a cup on a windowsill.

You look the stranger in the eye, and you say, There's no coffee in this house, friend. The Sultan has forbidden it.

He looks at you for a long moment. His calm, dark eyes don't blink. Then he smiles, very slightly, and nods, and walks away down the street. The man in the shadows follows him.

You close the door. Your hands are shaking. You don't sleep at all that night. You sit by the window until dawn, listening for footsteps.

The next morning, there's a commotion three doors down. Your neighbour, a cheerful old man who sold pipes, is gone. He used to slip you sugared almonds when you were a boy. They say a traveller knocked on his door late last night, asking for a smoke. They say your neighbour was a kind man, and couldn't say no.

And they say the traveller had a neat black beard and calm, dark eyes.

You sit on your doorstep for the rest of the day and don't move.

You keep thinking about how close it was. One word. Yes. That's all it would have taken. You saved your own life by doing the one thing you hate most in the world. Turning down a cup of coffee.

That winter, your mother dies, quietly, in her sleep. Just like your father. The house is very empty after that.

And here's the part nobody tells you.

Three years later, in February 1640, Sultan Murad the Fourth dies. He's only 27 years old. The doctors say it's a disease of the liver. Everyone in Istanbul knows what that means, though nobody says it out loud.

The Sultan who banned wine drank himself to death.

Within a few years, the coffeehouses come back. Slowly at first, then everywhere. The bans fade away. By the time you're an old man, Istanbul has more coffeehouses than ever.

And you open one.

It has a blue door, six cushions along the wall, and a copper pot that you dig up from your mother's garden, green with age, and polish until it shines. On opening night, you roast the beans until they crackle like rain on a roof. You boil the coffee three times, with a pinch of cardamom, until the foam rises on top.

The first cup, you pour for an empty cushion by the wall. On it, you place a handkerchief and a wooden stick.

Then you tap the stick on the floor, the room goes silent, and you start to tell a story. It's about a fisherman who catches a magic fish. You do the voices. The pasha. The camel. The ghost.

And at the most exciting moment, you stop, sip your coffee, and say, come back tomorrow.

And they do.

Coffee doesn't stay in Istanbul, either. Ottoman merchants and diplomats carry it west. Within a few decades, coffeehouses open in Venice, in London, in Paris, in Vienna. In London, they become so popular that people call them penny universities, because for the price of a cup, you can sit and listen to the cleverest people in the city argue. Schools of the wise, all over again.

Today, people around the world drink around two billion cups of coffee every single day. Turkish coffee, the kind you made in your little copper pot, is now protected by UNESCO as part of the world's cultural heritage. There are coffee shops on every street corner, in every airport, in every city on earth.

And nobody has ever been sewn into a bag for ordering a latte.
